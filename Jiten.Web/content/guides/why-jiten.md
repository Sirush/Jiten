There are a lot of good tools for learning Japanese, and Jiten is built to work with them rather than replace your setup. This is a short look at what it focuses on, so you can find out how it'll fit your workflow.

## Built around real media

Jiten starts from the things you actually want to read and watch. Anime,  novels, manga, visual novels, video games, and more, each analysed and turned into a vocabulary list along with stats. New titles are added constantly. You can see the latest additions on the [media updates page](/media-updates).

Instead of working through an abstract "core6k" deck, you study the words of *this* show or *this* novel, and learn the words you'll actually meet.

## Coverage you can trust

The most useful number on the site is **coverage**: the share of a title's words you already know. It is worked out against your own knowledge, so you can open any media in the library and see how comfortable it will be *for you* before committing several hours to it. Knowing when you're ready for it is one of the hardest part of immersion learning, and coverage makes this question easier to solve. [Choosing media at your level](/guides/choosing-media-at-your-level) covers strategies to find appropriate media for you.

## Difficulty you can compare

Every title gets a difficulty score from a model trained on Japanese text, adjusted by votes from readers who have actually finished it. That gives you a consistent scale to compare titles on instead of guessing from a synopsis.

It is a comparison within a kind of media rather than across all of them. Comparing two novels, or two anime, is what the score is good at, as asking whether a novel is harder than an anime is often a question that's impossible to answer.

## Free, open, and yours

The core is free and stays free: the library, coverage, deck downloads, the SRS and the [Jiten Reader extension](/guides/browser-extension). [Jiten+](/jiten-plus) exists for people who want to chip in, and adds extras on top: images and audio on cards, custom frequency lists, immersion plans, your coverage over time, monthly boosts for media requests, and higher limits.

The project is [open source](https://github.com/Sirush/Jiten), and the data it derives, decks, frequency lists and statistics, is licensed under CC BY-SA 4.0. See [deck licensing](/guides/deck-licensing) for what that covers and what it does not.

Your data is yours in a practical sense. You can export decks as Anki, CSV or plain text, download frequency dictionaries, and take a **Complete Backup** of your entire vocabulary with its review history, stability, difficulty and due dates. Leaving is always one export away, which is how it should be.

## It plays well with others

You do not have to abandon your setup to use Jiten.

**Anki** works in both directions: export a deck as `.apkg`, or import your studied cards through AnkiConnect and bring their review history with them. **Yomitan** works three ways: [download Jiten's frequency dictionaries](/guides/yomitan-frequency-dictionaries), export a per-title occurrence dictionary, or [load your own Yomitan dictionaries into Jiten](/guides/custom-yomitan-dictionaries) so your preferred definitions show up across the site in that browser.

For texthookers, manga readers and video players, the [Jiten Reader extension](/guides/browser-extension) parses the page and syncs everything back to your account.

Coming from somewhere else, your can import your known words [in a few clicks](/guides/tracking-known-words).
